Extra English prose for the fp8 KV calibration corpus. Original text written for this file; it is
deliberately NOT drawn from tools/reference/kl_corpus/, which is the held-out corpus the KL report
is measured on, and not from any of the repository's own source or documentation files.

A lighthouse keeper on the northern coast once kept two logbooks. The first recorded the weather:
wind from the northwest at thirty knots, visibility under a mile, swell running four metres and
building through the afternoon. The second recorded everything else -- the gull that nested in the
ventilation cowl for three summers running, the fishing boat that passed at the same hour every
Tuesday, the week in February when the fog was so complete that the lamp seemed to end a few
metres from the glass. Only the first logbook was ever collected by the harbour authority. The
second was found in a drawer forty years later, and it is the one people now read, because the
weather in the first is a table of numbers anyone could have written, while the second is the
record of a particular person paying attention to a particular place.

There is a lesson in that for anyone who measures things. A measurement is cheap to take and
expensive to interpret, and the interpretation is almost always where the value is. Consider the
ordinary business of timing a program. Anyone can report that a routine took one hundred and
twelve milliseconds; the number is real, repeatable to within a percent or two, and very nearly
useless on its own. What matters is whether those milliseconds were spent waiting on memory,
waiting on a lock, or doing arithmetic that the problem actually required, and no stopwatch will
tell you which. The stopwatch tells you that something is slow. It never tells you what to do.

The same asymmetry shows up in the humbler corners of engineering. A thermometer in a server room
reports twenty-four degrees; the interesting question is whether that reading came from the cold
aisle or from six inches above a card that is quietly throttling. A disk reports that it wrote
eight gigabytes; the interesting question is how many of those bytes will ever be read again. A
questionnaire reports that eighty-one percent of respondents were satisfied; the interesting
question is who declined to answer, and why. Each of these is a case where the instrument is
honest and the inference is where the difficulty lies.

Markets, oddly, have the same shape. A price is a single number that summarises an enormous amount
of disagreement, and the summary is useful precisely because it throws that disagreement away. Two
people trade at ninety-four dollars because one of them thinks the thing is worth more than that
and the other thinks it is worth less; the price records neither belief, only the point where they
briefly touched. When the price moves to ninety-seven, the temptation is to say that the thing
became more valuable, which is a statement about the world, when all that is strictly known is
that the balance of opinion shifted, which is a statement about people.

None of this is an argument against measuring. It is an argument for keeping the second logbook.
Numbers travel well and context does not, so the context has to be written down deliberately or it
is lost the moment the person who had it moves on. The best engineering notes I have read are
mostly prose with a few numbers in them, and they usually answer three questions that no dashboard
ever answers on its own: what was I worried about, what did I check, and what would have changed
my mind. Ten years later the dashboard is gone, the units may have changed, the thresholds are
certainly wrong -- and those three answers are still worth reading.

In the winter of 1871 a surveyor working a mountain valley recorded the same benchmark elevation
on four consecutive days and got four different answers, spanning about eleven centimetres. He
could have averaged them and moved on. Instead he wrote a page about the afternoon light, the
shimmer over the warm rock face, and his suspicion that the refraction of the sightline changed
with the heat of the day. He was right, and the correction he proposed was still being applied,
with his name on it, long after every one of his individual readings had been superseded. The
readings were data. The page was the contribution.
